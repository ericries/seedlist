## Background

Alex Salazar is co-founder and CEO of Arcade.dev, a San Francisco Bay Area company building the secure "action layer" for production AI agents [^1][^2]. He co-founded Arcade in 2024 with Sam Partee, a former Redis engineer [^3][^4]. Salazar is based in Burlingame, California [^5].

Salazar is a second-time founder in the developer-identity space. He previously co-founded Stormpath with Les Hazlewood; the company built what was described as the first authentication API for developers and was acquired by Okta on March 6, 2017 [^6][^7]. At Okta following the acquisition, Salazar served as VP of Product Management, where per Arcade and Laude Ventures materials he led developer products that grew to represent roughly 25% of Okta's total bookings, and later as GM launched an auth-centric proxy product (Okta Access Gateway) that reached about $9M in first-year revenue [^2][^8].

Salazar holds a B.S. in Computer Science from Georgia Institute of Technology and an M.B.A. from the Stanford Graduate School of Business, where he was president of the Entrepreneur Club [^1][^2].

Arcade itself has raised $72M to date: a $12M seed led by Laude Ventures in March 2025, and a $60M Series A led by SYN Ventures announced June 12, 2026 with strategic participation from Morgan Stanley and Wipro [^3][^9][^10].

## Stated Thesis

Salazar is being profiled here primarily as a founder and operator; he does not publicly market himself as an active angel investor. His public writing on the Arcade blog and his podcast interviews focus on where he thinks the AI agent stack is going: the constraint on production agents is not model capability but the "action layer" — how agents authenticate to services, take actions on behalf of users, and are governed by enterprise policy [^2][^11][^12].

In podcast and blog form, Salazar has publicly staked out positions on: the Model Context Protocol ("MCP Has Already Won," April 2, 2026), the tooling gap that separates AI demos from production ("Your AI demo is a lie"), and enterprise governance for autonomous agents [^2][^11][^12].

## In Their Own Words

On why large language models can't be trusted with credentials directly, from The Secure Developer podcast (Snyk, episode 161):

> "You cannot trust a large language model. You can't give it credentials, and you can't trust it to make the right decision, and you don't want to over-permission it because it might do a bunch of bad stuff." [^11]

On authentication in the agent era, same interview:

> "In agent world, that problem is easy piece. Now the problem is, how does the agent itself interact with authenticated and authorised services?" [^11]

> "The services that matter, the really valuable ones, it has to be on behalf of the end-user." [^11]

On hallucinations as a permanent constraint:

> "Hallucinations will always be a problem. We can mathematically prove to you that they will always be a problem, right?" [^11]

On the current moment for developers:

> "There couldn't be a better time to be a developer. Life just got so much easier, and the grind work has just been severely minimised." [^11]

From an Arcade blog post recounting the company's pivot into tooling infrastructure:

> "We started Arcade to solve a really different problem than what we're focused on today." [^15]

> "So we had to build something entirely new. In getting this agent to work, we created a new tooling layer for AI." [^15]

> "We'd created something that didn't just unblock our agent — it could unblock anyone trying to build an agent." [^15]

On plans for the Series A capital:

> "We're not slowing down. The $60 million is to accelerate: more tools, deeper governance, broader reach, and faster releases." [^9]
