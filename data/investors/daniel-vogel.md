## Background

Daniel Vogel is the co-founder and CEO of Bitso, the largest cryptocurrency exchange in Latin America [^1][^2]. He grew up in Mexico and studied at Stanford University, graduating in 2009 with a BA in Economics and a BS in Computer Systems Engineering [^2][^3]. He later earned an MBA from Harvard Business School in 2015 [^2][^3].

Before founding Bitso, Vogel worked as a software engineer at Quantcast in San Francisco, where he built a real-time bidding system that became central to the company's revenue [^3]. A colleague at Quantcast introduced him to Bitcoin, sparking a fascination he described in an interview: "After reading Bitcoin's white paper back in 2010, I was fascinated with the idea that there was no need for a centralized system in order to create monetary value" [^4].

Vogel co-founded Bitso in 2014 alongside Pablo González and Ben Peters, whom he met while pursuing his MBA at Harvard [^4][^5]. Under his leadership, Bitso raised a $250 million Series C in May 2021 at a $2.2 billion valuation, co-led by Tiger Global and Coatue, with participation from Paradigm, BOND, Valor Capital Group, QED, Pantera Capital, and Kaszek — making Bitso the first crypto unicorn in Latin America [^6][^7].

Vogel has been recognized as an MIT Technology Review "Innovators Under 35 Mexico" (2016), a MassChallenge Gold Winner (2016), a "Digital Leader Mexico" designation from the Mexican government, and a Global Endeavor Entrepreneur [^3]. He co-founded Fintech México, the industry association, and is a Partner at Xochi Ventures, a member-led early-stage venture fund focused on Latin America and the US [^1][^8][^9].

## Stated Thesis

Vogel has not published a formal individual investment thesis. His public statements emphasize crypto as financial infrastructure for Latin America rather than as speculative asset. In the announcement of Bitso's Series C, he stated: "In some parts of the world, crypto is associated with speculation. Bitso's customers rely on the technology for everyday uses from receiving remittances to engaging in international commerce" [^6].

As an investor, his most consistent public frame is that stablecoin-based payment rails are the natural architecture for global commerce. He has said: "If global commerce didn't exist and it would start existing today, and someone were to say, how do I build a payments network... it would need to be on a stable coin. No one would invent correspondent banking" [^10].

Xochi Ventures, where he is one of twelve partners, describes itself as "Latin America's premier Venture Capital Fund," targeting seed-stage companies transforming Latin America [^8]. According to Tracxn, Xochi's typical seed check size averages approximately $4.5M, with a stated range of $100K to $5M and a "sweet spot" around $2.5M [^9][^11].

## Inferred Thesis

**Sample size caveat:** Vogel's individual angel and Xochi-attributed investment record is small — Tracxn lists roughly seven angel investments in his personal portfolio, and Signal by NFX shows six with named rounds [^11][^12]. Of these, only two rounds have been independently confirmed via primary press sources (Jeeves Series A and Nowports Series C). The sample is too small to compute meaningful percentages; qualitative patterns follow.

Based on the verified and secondary-source portfolio (7 companies), the following patterns hold:

- **Sector:** Almost exclusively fintech and adjacent enterprise software. Jeeves (corporate expense/cross-border), Clara (corporate cards, Mexico), Nowports (digital freight forwarder, LatAm), Reworth (transaction-data fintech), Cubo (SMB fintech), Gondi (fintech infrastructure). Latitud and Wemerang are the outliers (venture platform and consumer, respectively).
- **Geography:** Predominantly Latin America — Mexican and Brazilian founders dominate the list, with Miami-headquartered LatAm-facing companies (Jeeves) also represented.
- **Stage:** Skews seed and Series A. Nowports Series C is the exception and was a growth-round check via the SoftBank-led round.
- **Co-investor pattern:** He is repeatedly grouped with other LatAm operator-angels in the same rounds — David Vélez (Nubank), Carlos García Ottati (Kavak), Sebastián Mejía (Rappi), Florian Hagenbuch (Loft), Ricardo Amper (Incode), Alex Bouaziz (Deel), Roger Laughlin (Kavak) [^13][^14]. This suggests a LatAm founder-mafia dynamic where operator-CEOs invest together in each other's networks.
- **Delta vs. stated:** His public voice is heavily focused on crypto and stablecoins, but his actual angel portfolio is overwhelmingly non-crypto fintech and logistics. This is a meaningful gap: Vogel's angel activity looks more like a LatAm operator diversifying into the broader regional fintech ecosystem than a thesis-driven crypto investor.

## In Their Own Words

On making crypto useful (Bitso's mission): "Bitso offers products and services for individuals and businesses to use crypto in their everyday life" [^6].

On crypto's role in Latin America (Bitso Series C announcement, May 2021): "This is a pivotal moment for the future of finance in Latin America. We see a significant amount of traditional financial infrastructure in the region being replaced by crypto" [^6].

On stablecoins as payment infrastructure: "If global commerce didn't exist and it would start existing today, and someone were to say, how do I build a payments network... it would need to be on a stable coin. No one would invent correspondent banking" [^10].

On learning as a business practice: "The rate of learning is very important for the future of society, and the way in which you run your business, the way in which you educate yourself, your families" [^10].

On transparency inside Bitso: "We used to not be as transparent... people had no idea what was going on. They had no idea whether the business was profitable, whether it was not profitable" [^10].

On the drive to build: "Most people have dreams and they don't go after them, just because they don't have the drive to go after them... I think we are, most of the time, the limiting factor in achieving our dreams" [^10].

On his first exposure to Bitcoin: "The next thing I knew, it was daylight, and I had spent the whole night reading about Bitcoin" [^17].

On the early perception of crypto: "This is a scam; this is a pyramid scheme" — describing how his family and colleagues initially reacted to his interest in Bitcoin [^17].

On raising Bitso's earliest capital: "It was so difficult. The first time we did a fundraise, it was super hard" [^17].

On his motivation as a founder: "The thrill of starting things was always more about the thrill of doing something and less so about the thrill of starting a company" [^17].

On financial equality (2019 interview): "The least egalitarian products and services are the traditional financial products... This technology (crypto) is giving access to equal financial services, no matter who you are or what you have" [^4].

On the discovery of Bitcoin's use case (2019): "An exchange was what LatAm needed in order to enable crypto adoption in the region because it would allow people and businesses to convert Bitcoin into liquidity" [^4].
