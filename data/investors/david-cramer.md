## Background

David Cramer is the co-founder and Chief Product Officer of Sentry, the open-source application monitoring and error tracking platform [^1][^2]. A self-taught software engineer, he dropped out of high school in the 9th grade, managed a Burger King in Lincoln, Nebraska, and worked at gaming content companies in Germany and San Francisco before joining Disqus (for about three years) and then Dropbox [^2]. He has been based in San Francisco since roughly 2008 [^2].

Cramer started Sentry as a 70-line open-source side project in 2012, which grew into the most widely adopted crash-reporting platform in the world [^2][^3]. Sentry is used by over four million developers at more than 100,000 organizations — including Anthropic, Disney, Instacart, Duolingo, Slack, and Cursor — and was valued at over $3 billion as of 2022, having raised $217M from investors including Accel, NEA, and Bond Capital [^2][^3]. Cramer has cycled through the CEO and CTO roles at Sentry and now serves as Chief Product Officer; Dave Rosenthal joined as Sentry's CTO in May 2024 [^2][^4].

Outside of Sentry, Cramer is an active angel investor in developer tools, infrastructure, and AI companies, and is described by First Round as an angel investor in "Vercel, Laravel, Dandelion Chocolate, and dozens of other companies" [^2]. He writes at cra.mr and is active on X as @zeeg and on the fediverse at @zeeg@astrodon.social [^5].

## Stated Thesis

Cramer has not published a formal investment thesis, but his public writing and podcast appearances reveal a consistent worldview that shapes what he backs.

He treats open source as both a philosophy and a distribution strategy. He wrote in launching Sentry's Open Source Pledge that the goal was to "Pay maintainers, directly. Do it sustainably, and scale it with our growth" — and framed the pledge as: "You commit (with receipts) to giving 2,000 USD to your dependencies, per engineer you employ, every single year" [^6]. On why cash matters more than gratitude, he wrote that "giving cash money to maintainers is an appropriate way to show your thanks, to recognize their hard work" [^6]. He has also said "In choosing to be an open source company, you're choosing to support a community that believes in the right to learn" [^7].

On what makes a company work, he prioritizes distribution over product novelty: "Distribution is the thing that has no luck involved… 20% of our revenue comes from this product because we have distribution, not because it's the greatest product" [^2]. And he defines product-market fit narrowly: "Product market fit is when a customer emotionally, viscerally reacts in a positive way… not just 'that seems useful.'" [^2].

He has publicly stated that he has largely slowed his angel investing pace because he views the current AI investment environment as a bubble where valuations no longer make sense [^2][^8]. Despite that view, his most recent verified investments (2025–2026) are all in AI infrastructure or AI-adjacent developer tools — suggesting that when he does invest, he still concentrates in the sectors he already knew before the boom.

## Inferred Thesis

Based on 7 independently verified investments from primary sources (press releases, company announcements, and firm portfolio pages). Aggregator databases (CB Insights, Crunchbase) suggest 14–33 total angel investments depending on source [^8][^9]; this analysis covers roughly 20–50% of his likely total. Sample size is small — the patterns below should be read as qualitative, not statistical.

**Sector concentration:** Every one of the 7 verified investments is a developer tool, developer platform, or infrastructure company for engineers. There are no consumer, fintech, or healthcare positions in the verified set — Cramer invests almost exclusively in the category he built his own career in.

**Sub-sector breakdown of 7 verified investments:**
- Web/app framework and hosting (Vercel, Laravel) — 2 of 7
- Developer observability / product ops (PostHog, Modem) — 2 of 7
- CI/CD and build infrastructure (Blacksmith) — 1 of 7
- Browser infrastructure for AI agents (Kernel) — 1 of 7
- AI monitoring and control (White Circle) — 1 of 7

**Stage distribution (7 verified investments):**
- Pre-seed / seed: 4 of 7 — Modem (pre-seed), Blacksmith (seed prior to Series A), White Circle (seed), PostHog (seed)
- Series A: 2 of 7 — Kernel (Series A), Strapi (participated as angel; round type Series A)
- Later stage / undisclosed: 1 of 7 — Vercel (individual investor, round unspecified on Vercel's about page)

**Geographic pattern:** US-founded companies dominate the verified set (Vercel, PostHog, Blacksmith, Kernel), with two international positions: Laravel (Arkansas), White Circle (Paris), Modem (Toronto), and Strapi (French-founded). Cramer is willing to write checks outside the Bay Area — reasonable given his own portfolio experience with globally distributed open-source projects.

**Founder profile patterns:** Portfolio founders are technical engineers building for other engineers, and several are open-source project maintainers who commercialized their work (Taylor Otwell at Laravel; the Strapi team; PostHog founders). One notable pattern in the recent set: Modem was founded by Ben Vinegar (former founding engineer / VPE at Sentry), and Cramer co-invested alongside his Sentry co-founder Chris Jennings — indicating he backs former colleagues.

**Co-investor patterns:** In verified deals Cramer has appeared alongside Accel (Modem, Laravel — both leads), Google Ventures (Blacksmith lead), Y Combinator (Kernel), SV Angel (Kernel), Solomon Hykes (Kernel, PostHog), Paul Graham (Kernel), Chris Jennings (Modem, and Sentry co-founder), and Ivan Zhang of Cohere (Modem). The Kernel round in particular grouped Cramer with three other well-known developer-tools angels (Hykes, Graham, Cramer) — this cluster of ex-Docker / ex-YC / ex-Sentry angels appears repeatedly in dev-tools rounds.

**Check size:** No verified check-size disclosure; not included in frontmatter.

## In Their Own Words

**On distribution vs. product quality** (First Round Review podcast, 2025):
> "Distribution is the thing that has no luck involved… 20% of our revenue comes from this product because we have distribution, not because it's the greatest product." [^2]

**On product-market fit** (First Round Review podcast, 2025):
> "Product market fit is when a customer emotionally, viscerally reacts in a positive way… not just 'that seems useful.'" [^2]

**On common founder mistakes** (First Round Review podcast, 2025):
> "Build the business right away, monetize right away and then recognize if it's not gonna work." [^2]

**On founder selection** (First Round Review podcast, 2025):
> "I look at people that are successful in our organization. You gotta have the right capabilities and then you just gotta try really, really hard." [^2]

**On why Sentry launched the Open Source Pledge** (Sentry blog, October 2024):
> "Pay maintainers, directly. Do it sustainably, and scale it with our growth." [^6]

**On what open source companies actually owe upstream maintainers** (Sentry blog, October 2024):
> "Not a single one of those founders did anything more than talk about the problem." [^6]

**On the Pledge's dollar commitment** (Sentry blog, October 2024):
> "You commit (with receipts) to giving 2,000 USD to your dependencies, per engineer you employ, every single year." [^6]

**On the value proposition of paying maintainers directly** (Sentry blog, October 2024):
> "giving cash money to maintainers is an appropriate way to show your thanks, to recognize their hard work" [^6]

**On the philosophy of running an open-source company** (CIO interview):
> "In choosing to be an open source company, you're choosing to support a community that believes in the right to learn." [^7]

**On approaching business** (CIO interview):
> "Approach your business like it's a consumer product, even if what you're building is not." [^7]

**On why he built Sentry** (CIO interview):
> "I found it annoying to deal with the systems admin on the team to get access to logs when I broke something." [^7]

**On motivation and adoption** (Orb "Tractable" interview):
> "I think useful things get adoption and we saw some of that where people wanted what it meant built and I'm the kind of persona that if there's adoption, I actually want to work on it. It's like a motivating factor for me." [^19]
