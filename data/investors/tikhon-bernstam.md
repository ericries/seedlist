## Background

Tikhon Bernstam is an American Internet entrepreneur and venture investor best known for co-founding Scribd (2006) and Parse (2011), the latter acquired by Facebook in 2013 [^1]. He was born in 1979 and grew up in Palo Alto, California [^1]. He attended Dartmouth College, studying economics, computer science, and physics, and graduated summa cum laude [^1].

In 2006, Bernstam co-founded Scribd with Trip Adler and Jared Friedman through Y Combinator's Summer 2006 batch [^1]. Scribd became a large document-sharing and subscription platform. In 2011, he returned to Y Combinator (Summer 2011) to co-found Parse alongside Ilya Sukhar, James Yu, and Kevin Lacker [^2]. Parse offered mobile back-end tools including cloud storage, identity management, push notifications, and custom code execution [^2]. Facebook acquired Parse on April 25, 2013 for $85 million [^2]. Business Insider named Bernstam one of the "top 15 CEOs to watch" in 2012 [^1].

Following the Parse acquisition, Bernstam invested full-time as an angel [^3]. In 2021, he co-founded Uncommon Capital with Jamie Quint (a former growth executive at Notion and Reddit), which invests at pre-seed through Series A in software, marketplaces, developer tools, fintech, and other categories [^4][^11]. He advises portfolio companies on engineering hiring, engineering culture, and fundraising strategy [^11]. Bernstam also holds advisor roles at Dynaboard, BalkanID, and Zight, and serves as a director at Jhana Education [^12].

## Stated Thesis

Bernstam has articulated a founder-first, perseverance-driven investment style rather than a formal sector thesis. In an interview with Techsauce, he described what he looks for in founders: "What we had was perseverance. So the first thing I look for in a startup are people that won't give up" [^5]. He has said he prioritizes founder focus over founder pedigree: "I look for people who have a maniacal focus on their business" [^5], and "The best founders won't tolerate distractions" [^5].

At Uncommon Capital, the stated investment scope is "B2B SaaS, marketplaces, consumer non-social, developer tools, and fintech," with hands-on operational support in product, data infrastructure, marketing, and engineering recruitment [^11].

Bernstam has also stated an unusual preference at his stage: "I like solo founders, though most do not" [^5], and looks for behavioral tells like responsiveness: "Small things like getting back on emails quickly. This is correlated with successful founders" [^5].

## Inferred Thesis

Portfolio data on Bernstam's personal angel activity is fragmentary across aggregators. NFX Signal lists 40 investments with 38 detailed [^6]; Tracxn lists 14 with rounds documented [^7]; PitchBook has reported approximately 73 total investments [^8]. This analysis is based on a smaller subset of investments with independently confirmable round data, plus Uncommon Capital's stated focus. **Sample size is small; percentages are not computed.**

**Sector orientation (qualitative):** Verifiable investments concentrate in developer tools and infrastructure (Linear, Parse — as founder, LemonEdge, MergeQueue, Aviator, Zight, Citusdata, Swiftype, Embrace), fintech (Caliza, Yendo, Kick, Secured Finance), and consumer/prosumer software (Scentbird, Crowdtilt). Uncommon Capital's headline portfolio companies — Razorpay, Talkdesk, Function of Beauty — reinforce the same three buckets: fintech infrastructure, developer/SaaS, and consumer subscription [^11].

**Stage:** Predominantly pre-seed and seed. Of the eight Signal-listed rounds with stage data, seven are seed or pre-seed and one is a Series A [^6].

**Geography:** San Francisco Bay Area concentration with select international bets (Razorpay in India, Caliza in Latin America) [^7][^11].

**Check size:** Signal NFX and Waveup report Uncommon Capital's historical average check at $2.5M, with a $30M maximum [^11]. Personal angel checks are smaller and undisclosed as a specific range.

**Co-investor patterns:** Bernstam has co-invested with Sequoia Capital, Index Ventures, Andreessen Horowitz, and Y Combinator on multiple rounds, most clearly documented in Linear's seed [^9].

**Notable gap between stated and inferred:** Bernstam describes a preference for solo founders [^5], but the largest confirmable investments (Linear, Razorpay, Function of Beauty) are all multi-founder teams. This is consistent with his broader statement that he cares more about perseverance and focus than team composition [^5].

## In Their Own Words

**On what he looks for in founders:**

> "What we had was perseverance. So the first thing I look for in a startup are people that won't give up." [^5]

> "I look for people who have a maniacal focus on their business." [^5]

> "The best founders won't tolerate distractions. They feel backed into a corner." [^5]

> "I like founders who are generous." [^5]

> "Small things like getting back on emails quickly. This is correlated with successful founders." [^5]

> "I like solo founders, though most do not." [^5]

**On markets and competition:**

> "It's better to go into a crowded market with garbage products and build something 10x better." [^5]

**On fundraising and control:**

> "Valuation is temporary, control is forever." [^10]

> "It's better to own 10% of a winner than 90% of a zero. Act accordingly." [^10]

> "Do not give up a board seat until a VC Series A. Never in a seed round." [^10]

> "The CEO's #1 job is to make sure the company doesn't run out of money." [^10]

**On employees and equity:**

> "Be very generous with equity and especially with titles." [^10]

> "Treat people well, and they'll fight for your company 'til death. Treat them badly, and they might cause its death." [^10]

**On the Parse sale to Facebook:**

> "The sale was a very contentious process and a very long struggle." [^13]

> "We had a lot of offers but Facebook made the most sense." [^5]

> "They cared about what we did, we respected the management. We got lucky." [^5]
